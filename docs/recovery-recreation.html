Long-term resilience

Preserve enough to rebuild the ecosystem later.

RLB's long-term goal is not merely to copy an SD card. It is to retain the content, device identity, firmware knowledge, storage layout and deployment evidence needed to recreate a retro-gaming environment if the original media or device is lost.

Core question: if the original card fails tomorrow, do you still know what the device was, what belonged on it, and how to build a replacement safely?

Recovery needs more than ROMs

A useful recovery record can include device identity, preservation reports, firmware relationships, frontend information, storage layout, BIOS, media, saves, configuration, hardware-specific boot assets and deployment history. Losing any one of those may make a future rebuild harder even when the game files survived.

Device knowledge supports reconstruction

Device Inspector is designed to detect, identify, preserve, fingerprint, analyze and recommend before deployment. That evidence can distinguish similar-looking devices and preserve details such as DTB or panel relationships that may matter when selecting a future build.

Deployment history matters

RLB treats build profiles and deployment history as managed device information. A successful build is useful evidence: it records which target process worked for a known device instead of forcing every future replacement to start from zero.

Verification makes recovery believable

Recovery is only as useful as the evidence behind it. RLB therefore emphasizes verification checkpoints and preservation accounting so a later rebuild can rely on known results instead of assumptions about what might have been copied.

Recovery is not firmware redistribution

RLB can preserve knowledge about firmware, compatible releases and deployment requirements without bundling commercial ROM collections or pretending to own upstream firmware projects. Provenance remains part of the recovery story.
